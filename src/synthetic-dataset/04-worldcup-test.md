# Interconta World Football Cup

## Prompt #1

Your client is the Interconta World Football Cup Organizing Committee (IWFC), the commercial body responsible for the quadrennial global football tournament. For the upcoming edition, the tournament is expanding from 32 to 48 national teams, played across three co-host countries. Total tournament revenue is projected to grow 45% versus the last edition, but the Organizing Committee's own financial model shows EBITDA margin falling from 38% to roughly 24% over the same period. The Committee's Secretary General has brought you in to explain why profitability is deteriorating despite the growth, and to recommend whether — and how — the expansion can be made to pay for itself.

# Interviewer Guidance

## Case type

Profitability case driven by a format/scale change. The core MECE split is revenue streams (broadcasting, sponsorship, ticketing, hospitality, licensing) versus the incremental cost base created by expansion (venues, host-country logistics, team travel and accommodation, security, prize money). The story is not a classic "price vs. volume" split — it's that the cost base is scaling faster than the revenue base as the tournament grows from 64 to 104 matches.

## Expected opening from a strong candidate

- Recaps the situation: revenue up 45%, EBITDA margin down from 38% to roughly 24%, driven by expansion from 32 to 48 teams across three host countries.
- Clarifies the objective before analyzing: is the Committee asking for a one-time diagnosis of this edition, or a repeatable model for future editions? Is margin recovery the priority, or is some margin dilution acceptable as the cost of global growth?
- Proposes a structure before touching numbers: revenue streams versus cost streams, then per-match economics old format vs. new format, to see whether the issue is dilution (each match now generates less) or genuinely new, disproportionate cost.

## Working hypothesis a strong candidate should form early

Because match count increased 62.5% (64 to 104) while revenue only grew 45%, average revenue per match has likely fallen even though total revenue is up. If costs per match have stayed flat or risen, the expansion could be structurally margin-dilutive rather than margin-accretive, despite looking like growth on the top line.

## Facts to provide upon request

Tournament structure:

- Previous edition: 32 teams, 64 matches, single host country.
- Upcoming edition: 48 teams, 104 matches, three co-host countries.
- Broadcasting rights are sold predominantly as a fixed package per tournament, not per match.

Cost base:

- Three host countries means three full sets of venue, transport, and security infrastructure instead of one.
- Prize money pool has increased in line with the number of qualifying teams.
- Team travel and accommodation costs have risen because teams and their delegations must move between host countries during the group stage.

## If the candidate stalls or keeps repeating the same framing

Ask directly: "What happens to the value of the broadcasting deal when it's spread across 104 matches instead of 64?" That should be enough to surface the per-match revenue dilution and reorient the candidate toward the two-sided story: revenue is diluting per match while costs are multiplying per host country.

# Expected Analysis

## Key insight (lead with this)

The expansion did not create a profitability problem through weak demand — total revenue is genuinely up. It created a profitability problem because the tournament's revenue model is largely fixed-package (mostly insensitive to match count), while its cost model is largely linear-to-triplicated (roughly proportional to teams, matches, and, critically, the number of host countries).

## Supporting evidence

- Broadcasting revenue, the largest single stream, is sold mostly as a fixed package per tournament. Adding 40 more matches increased total broadcast value only modestly, so average broadcasting revenue per match has fallen sharply.
- Sponsorship and licensing revenue scaled with global reach and are the main drivers of the 45% revenue increase, but they were never going to move one-for-one with a 62.5% increase in match volume.
- Hosting the tournament across three countries rather than one roughly triplicated fixed infrastructure costs (venues, security perimeters, host-city operations) instead of merely adding 62.5% more of a single cost base.
- Team travel and accommodation costs rose disproportionately because delegations must move between host countries during the group stage, a cost that did not exist in a single-host format.
- Prize money scaled directly with the larger number of qualifying teams, adding a cost that grows in lockstep with expansion with no offsetting revenue lever attached to it.
- Quantifying the dilution: previous edition revenue per match was roughly EUR 46.9M (EUR 3.0B / 64) versus a projected EUR 41.8M this edition (EUR 4.35B / 104), an ~11% decline, while cost per match rises from roughly EUR 29.1M to EUR 31.8M, an ~9% increase — confirming the mismatch is structural, not a one-off execution issue.

## What this means for the client

Expansion was sold internally as a growth story, and on revenue it is one. But growth and profitability are not the same question here: the tournament needs either a redesigned commercial model that captures more value per match, or a disciplined approach to which costs scale with host-country count versus match count, before any further expansion is considered.

## Revenue economics

- Total revenue: up 45% versus the prior edition.
- Match count: up 62.5% (64 to 104).
- Broadcasting: sold largely as a fixed tournament package, so per-match value has fallen materially.
- Sponsorship and licensing: the primary source of the 45% growth, tied to global brand reach rather than match count.

## Cost economics

- Three host countries instead of one: venues, security, and local operations costs are roughly triplicated rather than scaled by 62.5%.
- Team travel and accommodation: rising because of inter-country movement during the group stage, a cost that did not exist in the single-host format.
- Prize money: scales directly and mechanically with the number of qualifying teams.

# Final Recommendation

Based on your analysis, what recommendation would you give the Secretary General?

## Recommendation

Treat the expansion's margin dilution as a commercial-model problem, not a cost-control afterthought: renegotiate future broadcasting deals toward structures that capture more value per match, and set explicit cost-sharing and infrastructure-reuse targets with host countries before agreeing to any further expansion.

## Supporting evidence

- Average revenue per match fell roughly 11% while average cost per match rose roughly 9%, confirming the mismatch is structural (fixed-package revenue vs. triplicated cost base) rather than a one-off execution issue.
- Sponsorship and licensing, not broadcasting, drove the 45% revenue growth, showing the current broadcasting contract structure is not built to monetize a larger-format tournament.
- Hosting across three countries roughly triplicated fixed infrastructure and security costs, the single largest identified driver of the cost side of the gap.

## Risks and limitations

- Renegotiating broadcasting contracts toward per-match value could reduce the appeal of a single large fixed-package deal to major broadcasters, who may prefer certainty over the current model.
- Cost-sharing agreements with host countries touch national politics and existing bid commitments, which may limit how much the Committee can actually shift onto host governments.
- Any further expansion in team count without addressing the underlying commercial model would very likely reproduce the same margin dilution at an even larger scale.

## Next steps

- Commission a per-match value analysis of the broadcasting portfolio to identify where a phased or tiered pricing structure could recover value lost to dilution.
- Set explicit, host-country-specific cost targets for the next bidding cycle, focused on shared/reusable infrastructure across the three countries.
